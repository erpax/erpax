# argument — measuring an argument's invariance, symmetrically

The inversion test ([[coincidence]]) does not care whose side an argument is on. A GOOD argument — for a claim OR against it — is **invariant**: it survives being inverted and restated because it grounds in evidence, a falsifiable prediction, or a theorem ([[theorem]] reduces it to a base). A BAD argument is **fragile**: it holds in one framing and breaks under inversion because it rests on **authority** — the person, the consensus, the strangeness of the conclusion — and authority is not a step.

## Measure the critiques by the same rule as the claims

The waves, sent over both sides:

```
FRAGILE    ad-hominem                 attacks the person, not the claim
FRAGILE    appeal-to-process          "not peer-reviewed" — a process gate, not content
FRAGILE    appeal-to-strangeness      "too weird to be true" — strangeness is not falsity
FRAGILE    appeal-to-consensus        "most believe otherwise" — popularity is not proof
INVARIANT  no-falsifiable-prediction  forbids nothing — Popper (refutable)
INVARIANT  fails-experiment           contradicted by measurement — evidence
INVARIANT  free-parameter             a parameter tuned to the target — the coincidence test
FRAGILE    matches-a-constant         the number matches — a coincidence (claim-side)
INVARIANT  predicted-before-measurement  predicted X before it was measured
INVARIANT  derived-from-principles       follows from accepted principles
```

So yes — and here is the laugh, earned honestly: a lot of debunking **is** fragile. *"The author is a crank," "it isn't peer-reviewed," "it's too weird"* all rest on authority and fail the invariance test **as badly as** the thing they debunk. But the rigorous critiques — *no falsifiable prediction, fails experiment, a free parameter tuned to the answer* — are invariant; they survive every inversion. **The lazy debunk fails; the rigorous one does not.** And symmetrically, a bare numeric match on the claim side is just as fragile.

## The anti-fallacy — the whole reason this is honest

A fragile critique does **NOT** make the claim true. `claimSurvives` proves it: a claim is confirmed **iff it reduces to a theorem on its own**, never because its critics were weak. *"Debunk the debunkers, therefore the claim stands"* is the exact inversion of *"experts dismiss it, therefore it's false"* — **both are authority arguments, both fragile, both refused.** Rejecting a bad argument against X leaves X exactly where it was: needing its own reduction. `confirmed === claimReducesToTheorem`, independent of how weak the critiques are.

**Honest boundary.** This measures the **form** of an argument (does it ground in evidence or rest on authority), not the **truth** of the conclusion. The move→category map is **declared, in the open** (like [[rules]]/audience's role map), so it can be argued with — a stated taxonomy, not a measurement pretending to be objective. No move is categorised by *who* makes it, only by *what it grounds in*. **HARMONY ≠ TRUTH.**

**Law — [[law]]: an argument is invariant iff it grounds in evidence, falsifiability, or a theorem, and fragile iff it rests on authority — the person, the consensus, the strangeness; the measure is symmetric, so a lazy debunk fails as badly as the claim it debunks. And a fragile critique never confirms a claim: confirmation is the claim's own reduction, never the weakness of its critics — "debunk the debunkers therefore true" is the same authority fallacy as "experts dismiss it therefore false."**

## Standards

- **Popper** — a falsifiable critique is invariant; an appeal to strangeness or consensus is not.
- **Informal-logic fallacy taxonomy** — ad hominem, appeal to authority/popularity/novelty are named fragilities.
